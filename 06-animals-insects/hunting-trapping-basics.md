# Hunting & Trapping Basics

Starter overview. Legal/ethical hunting practice depends heavily on jurisdiction and situation — this covers general survival-context principles, not region-specific regulation (irrelevant in a true survival scenario, but worth knowing in normal times).

## General principles
- Trapping is generally more energy-efficient than active hunting — a set trap works while you do other tasks; this matters a great deal in a sustained survival/resource-constrained situation.
- Small game (rabbits, squirrels, birds) is more consistently available and easier to process than large game, and carries lower risk (large game hunting carries injury risk from the animal itself and from processing).
- Understanding local animal sign (tracks, scat, trails, feeding sign) is the foundation of both hunting and trapping — placement matters more than the specific trap/weapon design.

## Basic trap types (general concepts, not detailed build instructions)
- **Snares** — a noose set on an animal trail/den entrance, tightens when the animal moves through it; effective for small-to-medium game, requires knowledge of trail placement.
- **Deadfalls** — a weighted object triggered to fall on the animal; various trigger mechanisms (figure-4 trigger is a widely documented basic design).
- **Funnel/box traps** — direct animal movement toward a catch point using natural or constructed barriers; useful for both live-catch and lethal setups.

## Processing basics
- Field dress (remove internal organs) as soon as practical after a kill — delays increase spoilage risk and taint meat quality, especially in warm weather.
- Cool the carcass as quickly as possible; hang/skin promptly.
- Avoid gut contents contaminating meat during field dressing — this is the primary technique to get right.
- Cook thoroughly — wild game carries higher parasite risk than commercially processed meat in many regions (see `02-first-aid-medical/regional-health-considerations/` for regional disease patterns, some of which are animal-contact or bushmeat-associated).
