# Fuels Basics

## Wood
- The most universally accessible fuel in forested regions; seasoning (drying, typically 6-12+ months depending on species/climate) significantly improves burn efficiency and reduces smoke/creosote buildup versus burning green wood.
- Different species burn differently — generally, denser hardwoods burn longer and hotter than softer woods, which tend to burn faster and can produce more resin/creosote buildup in enclosed stoves/chimneys.

## Charcoal
- Produced by heating wood in a low-oxygen environment (pyrolysis) rather than burning it fully — burns hotter and cleaner than raw wood, useful for cooking and some craft/metalworking applications.
- Basic production method: a controlled, mostly-sealed burn (traditional earth mound or metal container methods both work) that lets wood smolder rather than fully combust, driving off volatile compounds and leaving carbon-rich charcoal behind.

## Biogas
- Produced by anaerobic digestion (bacterial breakdown without oxygen) of organic waste (manure, food scraps, other biodegradable material) in a sealed digester, producing mostly methane — usable for cooking or heating with appropriate burners.
- Requires a sealed digester vessel and a consistent supply of organic feedstock; more infrastructure-intensive to set up than wood/charcoal but turns waste into usable fuel and produces a nutrient-rich byproduct usable as fertilizer.

## Biodiesel
- Produced from vegetable oil (fresh or used) through a chemical process (transesterification, using a catalyst like lye and an alcohol like methanol) that converts the oil into a diesel-compatible fuel.
- Requires more chemical process knowledge and specific ingredients than the other fuels here — see `10-chemistry-materials/` as that folder develops for the underlying chemistry.
- Caustic chemicals involved (lye) require the same safety handling as in `05-plants-fungi`-adjacent... actually see general chemical safety principles once `10-chemistry-materials/` is built out — treat with real caution, not casual DIY handling.

## General fuel safety and storage
- Store fuel away from living/sleeping areas where practical, particularly anything flammable/combustible in quantity.
- Any enclosed-space fuel combustion (heating, cooking) needs deliberate ventilation planning — carbon monoxide poisoning from unventilated combustion is a real and serious risk, not a minor concern.
- Rotate stored fuel where it degrades over time (some liquid fuels degrade/gum up after extended storage) — know the shelf life of whatever fuel you're relying on.
