# Mechanical Power Basics

Power that doesn't route through electricity at all — often simpler to build and maintain than an electrical system, and worth knowing as a direct-use alternative (e.g. a water wheel directly turning a grinding stone, rather than generating electricity to run an electric grinder).

## Water power
- A **water wheel** (various designs — overshot, undershot, breastshot, differing in how water contacts the wheel) converts flowing/falling water directly into rotational mechanical power; works well for tasks like grinding grain or powering simple machinery directly via a shaft/gear connection.
- Site requirements: reliable water flow (volume) and/or fall height (head) — the specific wheel design should match what your site actually offers (a low-flow, high-head site favors different designs than a high-flow, low-head site).
- Can also drive a generator for electrical output (small hydro) rather than direct mechanical use — see `off-grid-electrical-basics.md`.

## Wind power (mechanical, not electrical)
- Traditional windmills convert wind directly to mechanical power (grinding, water pumping) without an electrical conversion step — often simpler and more robust than a wind turbine generator for a specific fixed task.
- Same siting consideration as electrical wind power: consistent average wind speed matters more than occasional strong gusts.

## Animal and human power
- A treadmill/treadwheel or a rotating harness setup can convert animal (or human) motion into rotational power for grinding, pumping, or other mechanical tasks — low-tech, doesn't depend on any fuel or weather condition, but limited in sustained power output and requires the animal/person's ongoing physical effort.
- Simple mechanical advantage tools (levers, pulleys, gear trains) can multiply human effort significantly for tasks like lifting or pumping water, worth understanding as a baseline even before considering powered alternatives.

## General principle
Before building an electrical system for a specific task (grinding grain, pumping water), consider whether a direct mechanical solution accomplishes the same task with less complexity and fewer failure points — electrical conversion adds efficiency losses and additional components that can fail.
