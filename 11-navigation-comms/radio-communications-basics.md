# Radio Communications Basics

## Why radio matters when other infrastructure fails
Cell networks and internet depend on extensive, powered infrastructure that can fail during a disaster or extended crisis; radio (especially battery/hand-crank/solar-powered) can function independently of that infrastructure, both for receiving information (news/weather/emergency broadcasts) and, with the right equipment and (in normal times) licensing, for two-way communication.

## Types of radio worth understanding
- **AM/FM broadcast receiver**: one-way, receive-only — for emergency broadcasts, news, weather; the most basic and widely accessible radio capability, worth having independent of power grid (battery, hand-crank, or solar powered).
- **NOAA weather radio (or regional equivalent)**: dedicated weather/emergency alert broadcasts in many countries — often has a special "alert" feature that activates automatically for urgent warnings.
- **CB (Citizens Band) radio**: short-range (typically a few miles, more with good conditions/antenna), no license required in many countries, useful for local coordination.
- **Amateur ("ham") radio**: significantly greater range and capability (local to global, depending on band and equipment), traditionally requires licensing and testing in normal times, but is the most capable option for genuine long-distance two-way emergency communication; learning the basics before a crisis (when licensing/testing is still functioning normally) is far more practical than trying to learn from scratch during one.
- **GMRS/FRS radios**: common consumer walkie-talkie type radios, short range, simple to use, useful for close-range family/group coordination (though GMRS technically requires a license in some countries, enforcement in a genuine emergency is a secondary concern to having working communication).

## Basic principles for effective radio use
- Antenna height and quality matter enormously for range — a better antenna on a modest radio often outperforms a better radio with a poor antenna.
- Keep transmissions brief and clear — congested emergency channels need efficient communication, not long conversations.
- Establish a family/group communication plan in advance: agreed channels/frequencies, check-in times, and a backup plan if primary radio contact fails — this is worth doing well before any emergency, not improvised during one.
- Learn to receive first — even without transmitting capability, monitoring emergency/weather channels provides valuable situational information.

## Needs deeper research
Specific frequency/band reference by region and radio type, antenna building instructions, amateur radio licensing study material (worth pursuing in normal times), Morse code as a fallback communication method.
