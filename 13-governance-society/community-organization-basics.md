# Community Organization Basics

## Why organization matters beyond the individual/family level
Many of the tasks described elsewhere in this archive (sanitation infrastructure, defense/security, larger construction projects, resource sharing) are more effective, or only possible, at a group scale larger than one household — community organization is what turns individual survival into a sustainable, rebuilding society.

## Basic decision-making models (each with real tradeoffs)
- **Direct democracy** (everyone votes on everything) — high legitimacy/buy-in, but slow and impractical past a fairly small group size.
- **Representative structures** (elected/chosen representatives make decisions on the group's behalf) — scales better than direct democracy, but depends on the representatives actually being accountable to the group, which requires some mechanism (elections, recall, term limits) to function well.
- **Consensus-based decision-making** — used successfully in many small, tightly-knit communities; requires more time/discussion than voting but tends to produce decisions everyone is genuinely willing to support, reducing the risk of decisions being quietly undermined by people who lost a vote.
- Different decisions may warrant different models — a small group deciding on an urgent, time-sensitive action needs a faster process than a group setting long-term shared rules.

## Roles a functioning community tends to need someone responsible for
- Coordination/leadership (whatever the decision-making model, someone or some body needs to actually organize and communicate decisions)
- Security/safety (see `01-quick-reference/emergency-type-triage.md` for individual-level guidance; group-level security coordination is a related but distinct need)
- Resource management (tracking and allocating shared supplies, land, tools)
- Dispute resolution (see `basic-law-conflict-resolution.md`)
- Health/medical coordination (who has relevant skills, where supplies are, triage priorities at a group level)
- Education (see `12-science-education/core-curriculum-outline.md`)

## Building trust and cohesion
- Shared work on visible, useful projects (building shared infrastructure, community meals) tends to build cohesion faster than abstract discussion alone.
- Transparent communication about scarce resources and decisions reduces suspicion and factionalism — secrecy about shared resources is a common source of community breakdown.
- Clear, fair processes (see `basic-law-conflict-resolution.md`) for handling violations of community norms prevent small conflicts from escalating into lasting divisions.

## Warning signs of community breakdown worth watching for
- Resource hoarding/secrecy, a collapse in willingness to share information, factions forming around perceived unequal treatment, and a breakdown in whatever dispute-resolution process was in place — addressing these early, openly, and through the agreed process is far easier than after trust has already broken down.
