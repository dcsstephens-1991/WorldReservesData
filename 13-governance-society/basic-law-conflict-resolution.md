# Basic Law & Conflict Resolution

General principles for maintaining order and resolving disputes within a group, independent of any specific national legal system that may or may not be functioning.

## Why written/agreed rules matter, even informally
- Groups without any agreed-upon rules for resolving disputes tend to resolve them through whoever has the most power/force — a written or at least clearly agreed set of basic rules, decided in advance rather than improvised mid-conflict, produces more consistent and more broadly accepted outcomes.
- Rules agreed to *before* a specific conflict arises are far more likely to be seen as fair by all parties than rules invented in the moment to resolve a specific dispute.

## Basic dispute resolution approaches
- **Mediation**: a neutral third party helps disputing parties reach their own agreement, without imposing a decision — generally preserves relationships better than an imposed ruling, works best when both parties are willing to negotiate in good faith.
- **Arbitration**: a neutral third party hears both sides and makes a binding decision — needed when mediation fails or when the parties can't agree even with help; requires the arbitrator to be seen as genuinely neutral to be accepted.
- **Restorative approaches**: focus on repairing harm (to the victim and the community) rather than only punishing the offender — often produces better long-term group cohesion than purely punitive approaches, especially in a small, interdependent group where everyone will need to keep working together.

## Basic principles for group rule-making
- Rules should be clear enough that people can know in advance whether an action violates them — vague rules invite unequal enforcement and disputes about interpretation.
- Some form of appeal or review process, even informal, reduces the risk of a single decision-maker's error or bias going unchecked.
- Proportionality — consequences should scale to the severity of the violation; consistently disproportionate consequences erode trust in the rule system itself.

## Common foundational legal concepts worth understanding
- **Property rights** — some agreed system for what belongs to whom reduces a major source of conflict; doesn't need to be complex, but needs to be clear and consistently applied.
- **Contracts/agreements** — a shared understanding that agreements (trade, labor, etc.) will generally be honored, and some method for addressing it when they aren't, underlies most cooperative economic activity (see `economics-trade-barter-basics.md`).
- **Due process concept** — the idea that someone accused of wrongdoing gets a fair chance to respond before a consequence is imposed — a widely-recognized principle across very different legal traditions, worth preserving even informally.
