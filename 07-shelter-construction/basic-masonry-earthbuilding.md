# Basic Masonry & Earthbuilding

Starter overview for building with stone, earth, and clay — durable, often fire-resistant, and usable with locally available materials in many regions.

## Cob (clay, sand, straw, water)
- A mix of clay-rich subsoil, sand, straw (or other fiber), and water, built up by hand in layers without forms.
- Basic soil test: a jar test (mix soil with water in a clear jar, let settle) roughly separates sand/silt/clay layers by particle size and weight, giving a rough sense of your soil's clay content before building.
- Good thermal mass (moderates indoor temperature swings), no specialized tools required, but slow to build and needs a dry climate or good roof overhang to avoid erosion.

## Adobe (sun-dried mud brick)
- Similar mix to cob (clay, sand, straw) but formed into bricks in molds and sun-dried before use, rather than built up wet in place.
- Faster wall construction once bricks are made (bricks can be pre-made in batches), traditional in arid/semi-arid climates worldwide.
- Same erosion vulnerability as cob — needs a roof overhang and/or a protective render (plaster) for longevity, especially in any rain.

## Rammed earth
- Damp subsoil (a specific moisture/clay ratio, not the same as cob) compacted in layers within a temporary formwork, producing a dense, wall-like monolithic structure.
- Requires more equipment (formwork, a tamper) than cob but produces a notably strong, dense wall.

## Stone masonry (dry-stack vs. mortared)
- **Dry-stack**: stones fitted together using gravity and shape alone, no mortar — requires more skill in stone selection/fitting, very durable, traditional in many regions for field walls and foundations.
- **Mortared**: stones bonded with a mortar (traditionally lime-based, or clay-based in some traditions) — allows less-perfectly-shaped stones to be used, generally faster for a beginner.

## General principles
- A stone or masonry foundation under a wood/earth structure meaningfully reduces moisture wicking into the main structure — worth prioritizing even in an otherwise wood-framed building.
- Fire resistance is a major advantage of masonry/earth construction over wood-only structures, relevant for cooking-fire-adjacent structures specifically.
