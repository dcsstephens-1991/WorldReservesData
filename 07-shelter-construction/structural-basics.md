# Structural Basics

Fundamental concepts for building something that stays standing and doesn't collapse on its occupants.

## Load paths — the core concept
Every structure needs a continuous path for weight (the structure's own weight, plus anything on/in it — people, snow load, wind) to travel from where it's applied down to the ground. A structural failure is usually a break somewhere in that path: a beam spanning too far without support, a wall opening (door/window) that removes support without compensating for it elsewhere, a foundation that can't bear the load above it.

## Foundations
- Purpose: spread the structure's weight over enough ground area that the soil can support it without sinking/shifting, and to keep the structure's wood/earth components away from ground moisture.
- Even a simple raised foundation (stone footings, or a wood structure raised on posts/piers) meaningfully extends a structure's life by reducing ground-moisture contact with load-bearing wood.
- Frost heave (soil expansion from freezing groundwater) is a real risk in cold climates — a foundation resting above the frost line can be pushed unevenly, cracking the structure above it; footings dug below the local frost depth avoid this.

## Roofing basics
- A roof needs adequate pitch (slope) to shed water/snow effectively — pitch requirements vary by roofing material and climate (heavy snow load areas generally need steeper pitches than the minimum needed just for rain).
- Roof overhangs protect walls (especially earth/masonry walls, per `basic-masonry-earthbuilding.md`) from rain erosion — a wider overhang is cheap insurance for wall longevity.
- Snow load: flat or low-pitch roofs in snow-prone regions need to be engineered/braced for significant accumulated weight, or actively cleared — this is a genuine collapse risk if underestimated.

## Openings (doors/windows)
- Removing material from a wall for an opening removes load-bearing capacity at that point — a header (a stronger beam spanning above the opening) is needed to redirect the load around the opening rather than through it.

## General safety principle
When in doubt about a structure's load capacity, over-build rather than under-build — the cost of extra material is almost always lower than the cost of a structural failure, especially for anything people will sleep under or spend significant time in.
