# Common, Widely-Distributed Survival Plants

These are plants with genuinely wide distribution, well-documented identification, and low ambiguity — chosen deliberately because they're hard to get dangerously wrong, unlike many wild foods. Still cross-check against a regional guide before relying on this alone, and see `docs/CURATION.md` for the two-independent-criteria rule this archive holds itself to for anything edibility-related.

## Cattail (*Typha* species)
- **Where**: marshes, pond/lake edges, ditches — nearly worldwide in temperate and subtropical wetlands.
- **ID**: tall (1-3m), strap-like leaves, unmistakable brown, sausage-shaped flower spike ("corndog" head) on a tall stalk — this seed head is highly distinctive and low-ambiguity.
- **Use**: young shoots (spring) peeled and eaten raw or cooked; rhizome (root) can be processed into a starchy flour by crushing and washing out the starch; pollen (summer) is a protein-rich flour additive.
- **Caution**: can absorb pollutants from contaminated water — avoid harvesting from water known to carry industrial/agricultural runoff.

## Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*)
- **Where**: essentially worldwide as a common "weed" in temperate climates — lawns, fields, disturbed ground.
- **ID**: basal rosette of jagged (toothed) leaves, single yellow composite flower per hollow stalk, later becoming the well-known white seed puffball — very low ambiguity, one of the most recognizable plants in the world.
- **Use**: leaves (best young, more bitter with age) raw or cooked; flowers edible; root can be roasted as a coffee substitute.
- **Caution**: avoid harvesting from areas treated with herbicide/pesticide, or roadside areas with vehicle pollution exposure.

## Broadleaf & narrowleaf plantain (*Plantago major* / *Plantago lanceolata* — not related to the banana-like "plantain" fruit)
- **Where**: worldwide as a common lawn/path "weed," extremely tolerant of foot traffic.
- **ID**: basal rosette of leaves with parallel veins running the length of the leaf (a genuinely distinguishing feature, low ambiguity), broad oval leaves (*major*) or narrow lance-shaped leaves (*lanceolata*), small greenish flower spike.
- **Use**: young leaves edible raw or cooked; also has a long folk history of topical use on minor cuts/stings/insect bites (crushed leaf applied directly) — reasonably well corroborated as a mild soothing/drawing agent, not a substitute for real wound care.

## Stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*)
- **Where**: temperate regions worldwide, disturbed/nitrogen-rich soil, forest edges, riverbanks.
- **ID**: opposite, serrated leaves covered in fine stinging hairs (causes an immediate, unmistakable stinging/itching reaction on contact — this is itself a strong, low-ambiguity identifying feature, if an unpleasant one to discover).
- **Use**: cooking or drying neutralizes the sting — young leaves are a nutritious cooked green, widely used traditionally.
- **Caution**: wear gloves when harvesting; do not eat raw.

## Chickweed (*Stellaria media*)
- **Where**: worldwide in temperate climates, common garden/lawn weed, cool-season growth.
- **ID**: small paired oval leaves on branching stems, tiny white star-shaped flowers with deeply notched petals (giving the appearance of ten petals when there are only five), a single line of fine hairs running down one side of the stem, alternating sides at each leaf node — this hair-line detail is a useful low-ambiguity confirming feature.
- **Use**: entire above-ground plant edible raw (mild, slightly grassy taste) or cooked.

## Oak acorns (*Quercus* species, worldwide temperate distribution)
- **Where**: oak trees are widespread across temperate Northern Hemisphere forests.
- **ID**: acorns (the nut in a cap) are unmistakable and essentially impossible to confuse with a toxic look-alike.
- **Use**: **not** safe to eat raw in quantity — most oak species' acorns are high in tannins, which are bitter and can cause digestive upset. Must be processed: shell, then leach the crushed or whole nutmeats in multiple changes of water (cold or hot leaching both work, hot is faster) until the bitterness is gone, then dry and grind into flour.
- **Caution**: this is a food-value/palatability issue (tannins), not a life-threatening toxin issue like the plants in `toxic-plants-widely-confused.md` — but skipping the leaching step produces a genuinely unpleasant, digestively harsh result, so don't skip it.
