# Medicinal Plants — General Principles

This file covers principles for using plants medicinally, not a catalog of specific remedies — herbal medicine is a domain where dosage, individual health conditions, and drug interactions matter enormously, and generic dosing advice from a general reference carries real risk.

## Core principles
1. **"Natural" does not mean "safe."** Many of the most potent, dangerous toxins known are plant-derived (see `toxic-plants-widely-confused.md` for examples like foxglove and castor bean) — a plant being traditional or wild does not make its dose-response curve forgiving.
2. **Dosage matters as much as the substance.** Many medicinal plants are the same plants that are toxic at higher doses — the difference between a helpful and a harmful outcome can be a small quantity difference that's hard to judge without training or reliable preparation standards.
3. **Drug interactions are a real risk**, especially for anyone on prescription medication (blood thinners, heart medication, diabetes medication are common interaction categories) — herbal treatment is not automatically safe to combine with pharmaceutical treatment.
4. **Pregnancy and children require extra caution** — many herbal remedies considered acceptable for a healthy adult are contraindicated in pregnancy or for children, at different thresholds than for adults.
5. **Positive identification is a prerequisite**, not optional — the same look-alike risks in `toxic-plants-widely-confused.md` apply directly to medicinal use; a misidentified "medicinal" plant is exactly as dangerous as a misidentified "edible" one.

## Reasonably well-corroborated, low-risk traditional uses (general categories, not specific dosing)
- **Topical use of plantain, aloe, or similar mild plants** on minor cuts/scrapes/insect bites — widely used traditionally, low risk when the plant is correctly identified, not a substitute for the wound care steps in `02-first-aid-medical/trauma-and-wound-care.md` for anything beyond minor.
- **Willow bark** as a traditional source of salicin (related to aspirin's active compound) — historically well-documented, but carries the same cautions as aspirin (bleeding risk, allergy, not for children due to Reye's syndrome risk with salicylates) plus the added uncertainty of variable concentration in a home preparation versus a standardized dose.
- **Ginger and peppermint** for mild nausea — widely used, low risk, low potency, reasonable first-line options when available.
- **Chamomile** for mild sleep/relaxation support — widely used, generally low risk for most adults, but allergy is possible especially for anyone with ragweed/related plant allergies.

## When to defer to pharmaceutical/professional treatment instead
- Any serious or worsening infection, significant injury, or condition covered in `02-first-aid-medical/medical-emergencies.md` — herbal treatment is not an adequate substitute for evacuation/advanced care in these situations, only a bridge when nothing else is available.
- Any situation involving a child, a pregnant person, or someone on regular medication, where interaction/dosing risk is elevated and general guidance is least reliable.

## Building this section out further
This file deliberately stays at the principles level. Specific regional medicinal plant catalogs, if added later, should be sourced from an authoritative regional reference (a recognized ethnobotanical text, a health ministry publication) and cross-checked, per `docs/CURATION.md` — not generated from general knowledge alone, given the dosage-sensitivity concern above.
