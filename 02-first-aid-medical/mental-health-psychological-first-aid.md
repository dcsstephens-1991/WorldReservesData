# Mental Health & Psychological First Aid

Crisis and disaster conditions cause serious psychological strain, on top of physical risk. This affects decision-making and group cohesion, not just individual wellbeing — treat it as a real first aid category.

## Psychological first aid — core principles (WHO framework: Look, Listen, Link)
1. **Look**: check for safety, for people with obvious signs of distress, and for basic needs (injury, safety, food/water/shelter) that need addressing first — psychological support can't substitute for unmet physical needs.
2. **Listen**: approach calmly, ask what they need, listen without pressuring them to talk about the traumatic event itself. Being present and calm is often more helpful than any specific technique.
3. **Link**: help connect people to practical support — information, family/social connection, basic services. Isolation worsens outcomes; help people reconnect with others.

## What helps in acute distress
- Calm, steady presence; simple, clear information (uncertainty increases distress).
- Basic needs first: safety, warmth, water, food, rest.
- Keep familiar routines/roles going where possible (chores, caregiving roles) — a sense of purpose and normalcy is protective.
- Reconnect people with family/social groups as soon as safely possible.
- Give people some sense of control/choice where possible, even small (what to eat, what task to do).

## What to avoid
- Forcing someone to recount traumatic details ("debriefing" immediately after trauma is not shown to help and can worsen outcomes).
- Dismissing or minimizing ("at least you're alive," "others have it worse") — acknowledge the difficulty instead.
- Over-promising ("everything will be fine") when you can't know that.
- Leaving distressed individuals isolated.

## Recognizing acute stress reactions (normal, usually time-limited)
Shock, denial, anxiety, anger, guilt, difficulty concentrating, sleep disruption, physical symptoms (headache, stomach upset) with no clear physical cause. Most people recover with time, safety, and support — this is not the same as a mental illness.

## When more urgent action is needed
- **Suicidal thoughts/expressed intent**: take seriously, do not leave the person alone, remove access to means if possible (weapons, medications), keep talking calmly, get them to any available mental health or medical support as soon as possible.
- **Psychosis** (disconnection from reality, hallucinations, severe confusion not explained by injury/fever): keep the person safe, calm, and in a low-stimulation environment; avoid confrontation over their beliefs; seek trained help if any is reachable.
- **Severe panic attack**: help them slow their breathing (in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 6-8), ground them in the present (name 5 things they can see, 4 they can hear, etc.), stay with them until it passes (usually 10-20 min).

## Supporting children
- Children take behavioral cues from calm adults — your own composure matters more than what you say.
- Keep routines and physical affection/reassurance consistent where possible.
- Answer questions honestly at an age-appropriate level; don't overshare details that add fear without helping them.
- Watch for regression (bedwetting, clinginess, tantrums) — normal short-term stress response in children, usually resolves with stability and support.

## Supporting caregivers/responders themselves
- Rotate physically and emotionally demanding tasks among available people where possible — sustained exposure without rest degrades judgment and increases error/injury risk.
- Basic self-care (sleep, food, water, brief breaks) is not optional for people in a caregiving/leadership role — it directly affects everyone depending on them.
- Peer support (checking in with each other) is one of the most effective, resource-free interventions available.

## Long-term / ongoing
- Grief, prolonged sadness, persistent anxiety, or trauma symptoms that don't improve over weeks-months may need more structured support when any mental health resource becomes reachable again — this file covers acute/field response, not treatment of ongoing conditions.

See `religious-cultural-considerations.md` — grief rituals, mourning practices, and beliefs about death vary significantly and matter a great deal to psychological wellbeing; support the family's practices rather than a single standard approach.
