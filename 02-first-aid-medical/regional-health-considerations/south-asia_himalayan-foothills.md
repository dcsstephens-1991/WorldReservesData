# Himalayan Foothills & High Altitude — Health Considerations

## Climate-driven risks
- Extreme cold at altitude, with rapid, unpredictable weather changes even within a single day — hypothermia and frostbite are the dominant climate risk here, opposite of most other subregions in this set.
- Landslide and avalanche risk on steep terrain, elevated during monsoon rains (lower elevations) and heavy snow periods (higher elevations).
- Thin air affects wound healing, exertion tolerance, and cold sensitivity — factor this into any injury response plan at altitude.

## Disease patterns
- **Altitude sickness (acute mountain sickness, and its severe forms HAPE/HACE)** — the leading medical concern above roughly 2,500m; ascend gradually (widely recommended: no more than ~300-500m net elevation gain in sleeping altitude per day above 3,000m), descend immediately at signs of severe symptoms (confusion, breathlessness at rest, loss of coordination) — this is a killer if ignored.
- **Waterborne illness (giardia and others)** — glacial-melt streams look pristine but still carry pathogens from upstream animal contact; treat all water regardless of apparent clarity.
- **Malaria** — largely absent at higher elevations, present in lower foothill valleys — don't assume elevation protects you at trailheads/lower valley towns.

## Dangerous species
See `06-animals-insects/` for identification detail. Fewer venomous snakes at higher elevation than the plains below:
- Himalayan pit viper at lower-to-mid elevations
- Himalayan black bear and snow leopard (rare encounters, but relevant in remote high-altitude travel)
- Leopards in lower foothill forest zones

## Notes
- Evacuation from high-altitude areas is often slow and weather-dependent (no reliable air support in poor conditions) — a conservative approach to ascent and injury risk matters more here than in lower-altitude subregions where evacuation is faster.
