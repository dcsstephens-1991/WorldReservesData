# East African Highlands — Health Considerations

## Climate-driven risks
- Elevation (much of this subregion sits 1,500–3,000m) gives a milder, more temperate climate than the surrounding lowlands — a notable exception within Sub-Saharan Africa. Nights can be cold even near the equator.
- Strong UV exposure due to elevation and low latitude combined — sunburn risk is higher than the mild temperatures suggest.
- Rift Valley areas and lower-elevation pockets within this subregion revert to lowland heat/malaria risk — don't assume highland conditions apply to every part of your route.

## Disease patterns
- **Malaria** — significantly reduced at higher elevation, but present in valleys, lakeshores, and lower-elevation pockets — check elevation-specific risk rather than assuming highland safety.
- **Typhoid** — reinforces water treatment and sanitation discipline, especially around dense population centers.
- **Rift Valley fever** — periodic, livestock-associated outbreaks (mosquito-borne and via contact with infected animal blood/fluids); avoid handling sick livestock during known outbreak periods.
- **Altitude-related illness** — relevant for rapid ascent to the higher peaks in this subregion (e.g. Kilimanjaro, Mount Kenya, the Rwenzoris); ascend gradually, watch for headache/nausea/dizziness that doesn't resolve with rest or slower pace.

## Dangerous species
See `06-animals-insects/` for identification detail. Named here for cross-reference:
- Black mamba, puff adder (widespread across elevation range)
- Hippos and crocodiles near lakes (Victoria, Turkana, Tanganyika) and rivers — among the most dangerous large animals to humans in the region
- Safari ants

## Notes
- Water sources at elevation look clean but still carry giardia and other pathogens from upstream livestock/wildlife contact — treat regardless of apparent clarity.
