# Metallurgy Basics

Chemistry/materials-science companion to `09-tools-manufacturing/blacksmithing-basics.md`, which covers the practical working of metal.

## Iron and steel — the core distinction
- **Iron** in its pure form is relatively soft; **steel** is iron alloyed with a controlled small amount of carbon (roughly 0.05-2% by common definitions), which significantly increases hardness and strength — this is why steel, not pure iron, is the material of choice for tools and blades.
- **Cast iron** has a higher carbon content than steel, making it hard but brittle — good for compression-loaded applications (cookware, some structural uses) but poor for anything needing to flex or resist impact without cracking.
- Too much carbon makes steel increasingly hard but also increasingly brittle — tool design balances hardness (edge retention) against toughness (resistance to chipping/breaking) based on the specific tool's use.

## Heat treatment — the chemistry behind it
- **Hardening**: heating steel above a specific transformation temperature (where its internal crystal structure changes) and then rapidly cooling (quenching, in water, oil, or another medium) locks the steel into a harder, but more brittle, crystal structure.
- **Tempering**: reheating hardened steel to a lower temperature (well below the original hardening temperature) reduces brittleness while retaining most of the added hardness, by allowing some internal stress relief — this is why the two-step process (harden, then temper) matters, as noted in `09-tools-manufacturing/blacksmithing-basics.md`.
- **Annealing**: heating steel and cooling it slowly (rather than quenching) softens it, useful for making steel easier to work/shape before a final hardening step, or for relieving internal stress in a finished piece.

## Corrosion (rusting) — the chemistry
Rust is iron oxidizing (reacting with oxygen and moisture) — the same basic oxidation chemistry as combustion, just far slower. Practical implications: keeping metal tools dry and lightly oiled meaningfully slows this process; some alloying elements (chromium, notably, in stainless steel) resist this reaction far better than plain steel, though stainless steel is harder to produce/work without industrial equipment.

## Other metals worth knowing as categories
- **Copper and bronze** (copper-tin alloy) — historically significant, workable at lower temperatures than iron/steel, good electrical/thermal conductivity, more resistant to corrosion than iron in many environments, but softer — less suited to edge tools, well suited to fittings, wiring, and cookware.
- **Aluminum** — light, corrosion-resistant, but requires quite high processing temperatures/technology to produce from ore; more realistic to reuse/recycle existing aluminum than to produce new aluminum from raw materials in a low-infrastructure setting.
