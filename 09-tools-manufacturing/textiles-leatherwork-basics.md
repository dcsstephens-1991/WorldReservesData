# Textiles & Leatherwork Basics

## Fiber sources
- **Animal**: wool (sheep, goat, alpaca and relatives), silk (from silkworm cocoons, more infrastructure-intensive to produce), various hair/fur fibers.
- **Plant**: cotton, flax (linen), hemp, and various other regional fiber plants — each has a different processing sequence to go from raw plant material to spinnable fiber.
- Fiber choice affects warmth (wool retains insulating properties even when damp, a genuinely useful cold-weather property that cotton lacks), durability, and processing complexity.

## Basic textile process chain
1. **Harvesting/shearing** the raw fiber material.
2. **Cleaning/processing** — removing debris, oils (in wool, this is scouring), or plant matter (retting, for plant fibers like flax, which involves controlled rotting to separate fiber from the woody stem).
3. **Carding/combing** — aligning fibers in preparation for spinning.
4. **Spinning** — twisting fibers into continuous thread/yarn, traditionally with a drop spindle (simple, portable, low-tech) or a spinning wheel (faster, more equipment-intensive).
5. **Weaving or knitting** — turning thread/yarn into fabric. A simple frame loom is a viable starting point for weaving without complex equipment.

## Basic mending and repair
- Hand-sewing (running stitch, backstitch, whip stitch) covers most repair needs without a machine.
- Darning (a woven patch technique for holes, especially in knitted fabric) extends the life of worn clothing significantly.
- Keep worn-out textiles as a raw material source (patches, stuffing, rags) rather than discarding — nothing goes to waste when replacement fabric is hard to come by.

## Leatherworking basics
- **Tanning** converts raw animal hide (which will rot) into stable, usable leather — traditional methods include vegetable tanning (using tannin-rich plant material, notably tree bark from certain species) and brain tanning (a traditional indigenous method using the animal's own brain tissue, which contains suitable fats/oils) — both are genuine, functional processes, though real detail on either requires more specific instruction than fits here.
- Basic leatherworking tools: an awl (for punching stitch holes), a sharp knife, and waxed thread cover most repair and basic construction needs (footwear repair, straps, containers).
- Leather is disproportionately valuable for durable, repairable items (footwear, tool sheaths, straps/harnesses) given how labor-intensive it is to produce from scratch — prioritize accordingly.
