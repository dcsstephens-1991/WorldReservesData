# Blacksmithing Basics

Working iron/steel by heating and shaping — the foundation of most other tool-making in a rebuilding scenario, since blacksmithing is how you make and repair the tools everything else depends on.

## Minimum viable setup
- **Forge**: a source of concentrated, sustained high heat — a simple coal/charcoal forge with a forced air supply (bellows or blower) reaches the temperatures needed for most blacksmithing work. Charcoal (see `08-energy-power/fuels-basics.md`) is a workable fuel where mineral coal isn't available.
- **Anvil**: a heavy, hard, flat-ish steel surface to work metal against — a large chunk of steel (even an improvised one, like a length of railroad rail) can substitute for a purpose-built anvil.
- **Hammer(s)**: at minimum one general-purpose forging hammer.
- **Tongs**: to hold hot metal safely — improvised tongs can be forged as an early, foundational project.
- **Quench container**: water or oil, for controlling cooling rate during heat treatment.

## Core process concepts
- **Heating to workable temperature**: steel becomes workable (malleable enough to shape by hammering) at high heat, indicated traditionally by color (dull red through orange to yellow as temperature increases) — color-based temperature judgment is a real, widely used skill, though it takes practice to calibrate.
- **Drawing out**: hammering to lengthen and thin a piece of metal.
- **Upsetting**: hammering to shorten and thicken a piece (the reverse of drawing out).
- **Bending, punching, splitting**: shaping techniques for specific forms (hooks, holes, forks/tines).
- **Heat treatment (hardening and tempering)**: heating steel to a specific temperature and then rapidly cooling (quenching) hardens it, but also makes it brittle; a subsequent tempering step (reheating to a lower temperature) reduces brittleness while retaining most of the hardness — both steps matter for a durable tool edge, skipping tempering produces a tool that chips/cracks easily.

## Safety
- Burns are the most common injury — treat every piece of metal near or in the forge as hot until proven otherwise.
- Eye protection matters, especially when hammering (scale/sparks fly off the workpiece).
- Ventilation matters for any enclosed forge space — combustion byproducts accumulate.
